MANIFESTO 001 / ON KNOWING & NOT KNOWING

A Manifesto for Epistemic
Resilience.

Our ability to produce information has grown faster than our ability to determine what deserves to be believed.

00 /

We live in a time when information is cheap.

We can generate more text, images, software, analysis, and explanations than anyone can realistically examine. AI has made this even easier.

But our ability to produce information has grown faster than our ability to determine what deserves to be believed.

We have built machines that can answer almost any question. We have not built enough systems that help us understand when those answers should be trusted.

This is not only an AI problem. It is a problem with how we produce, organize, verify, and use knowledge.

01

CLAIM

Plausibility is not knowledge

Plausibility is not knowledge.

A citation is not proof. A retrieved document is not understanding. A model's confidence is not evidence.

  • PLAUSIBLE ≠ TRUE
  • CITATION ≠ PROOF
  • CONFIDENCE ≠ EVIDENCE

02

BOUNDARY

Uncertainty is part of the answer

Most systems are built to produce answers. When information is missing or ambiguous, they tend to fill the gap. Sometimes the right answer is uncertainty — more evidence, competing explanations, or a system that asks for help or stops.

“We don’t know” is a valid result.

A system should not hide uncertainty simply because its interface expects an answer.

03

ARCHITECTURE

Don’t put every kind of judgment in one system

A single AI system can retrieve, interpret, reason, recommend, and act. Convenient — but mistakes become hard to understand or correct. We should be able to tell retrieval from interpretation, evidence from inference, and recommendation from action.

04

INTERFACE

Make important decisions challengeable

Systems gain authority through their interfaces. A confident answer looks more trustworthy than an uncertain one. For consequential decisions, people should be able to see:

  1. AWhat it concluded
  2. BEvidence used
  3. CAssumptions that mattered
  4. DWhat remains uncertain
  5. EWhat could change the result

Human agency requires meaningful challenge, not just human presence.

05

KNOWLEDGE MODEL

Knowledge is more than stored information

More documents do not automatically produce better decisions. Useful knowledge depends on the relationships between claims, evidence, assumptions, decisions, actions, and outcomes.

The future of knowledge software is not only better storage. It is better reasoning infrastructure.

06

FAILURE MODE

Build for correction

We will not build systems that never fail. We should build systems that can fail without becoming impossible to recover from.

  • TTraceable decisions
  • DDetectable errors
  • RRevisable assumptions
  • CContained or reversible action

The goal is systems that can recognize, explain, and recover from failure.

07

METRICS

Every optimization creates blind spots

Every system optimizes something — speed, growth, engagement, accuracy, automation, cost, throughput. Every metric also leaves something out.

08

AUTOMATION

Automate work, not necessary judgment

Automation is valuable when it removes unnecessary work and helps people decide better. It becomes a problem when it removes judgment that is still necessary.

  • RETRIEVE
  • COMPARE
  • SUMMARIZE
  • GENERATE HYPOTHESES
  • FIND PATTERNS
  • CHALLENGE ASSUMPTIONS

The goal is to make sure automation does not quietly remove responsibility where judgment still matters.

09

DEFINITION

What epistemic resilience means

Epistemic resilience is the ability of a system to remain useful and correctable when information is incomplete, conflicting, uncertain, or wrong.

THE LOOP MUST STAY WHOLE.

FORWARD = ACTION BACKWARD = TRACE

A resilient system keeps evidence → interpretation → decision → action → outcome → new evidence connected, distinguishes what was observed from what was inferred, and can be challenged and changed.

10

PROGRESS

A different measure of progress

The success of AI should not be measured only by how much it can generate or automate. We should also ask:

  1. Can people understand why a system reached an important conclusion?
  2. Can they challenge it?
  3. Can the system recognize when its evidence is insufficient?
  4. Can important errors be detected?
  5. Can decisions be reconstructed?
  6. Can new evidence change the result?
  7. Can responsibility remain visible?
  8. Can people still make meaningful decisions for themselves?

These are not secondary features. They are part of what makes an intelligent system reliable.

—

OUR PRINCIPLE

On what to build

We believe information without verification can become a liability. We believe automation without agency can create dependence. We believe optimization without reflection can make systems better at pursuing the wrong goal. And we believe intelligence without epistemic discipline can produce more plausible answers without producing better knowledge.

  • We should build systems that preserve evidence, represent uncertainty, expose important assumptions, support disagreement, enable verification, and make correction possible.
  • We should build systems that can be questioned.
  • We should build systems that can change their minds.
  • We should build systems that preserve the connection between evidence and action.
  • We should build systems that help people understand not only what an answer is, but how much confidence it deserves and what could make it wrong.

That is epistemic resilience.

Not the elimination of error, but the ability to remain oriented toward better understanding while error, uncertainty, and change are unavoidable.

Do not build systems that merely know more. Build systems that help us know what we know, recognize what we do not know, and act accordingly.
